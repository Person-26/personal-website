How this page is drawn

Everything you see here is light, simulated. The screen is a wave field, and the field is a mix of three frequencies: those of blue (460 nm), green (540 nm) and red (620 nm) light, slowed down so that the middle of the visible range would be 10 Hz. Three are enough, as for a laser projector: the eye has only three kinds of colour receptor. Each pixel's colour is what those frequencies look like together: how strongly each one oscillates there, seen through the eye's sensitivity to colour. The white background is all three at once; dark text is where there is no light.

The screen is a slice through the middle of a thin slab of space filled with that field. Every point in the slab is a harmonic oscillator coupled to its neighbours, which is all a wave is, and the simulation steps them forward in time, a few times a frame. Every frequency moves at the same speed, so each has its own wavelength. Around the screen a margin soaks up whatever reaches it, so waves leave as if into open space.

Nothing on the screen is drawn or driven directly. The only input is a boundary condition: on the two faces of the slab, above and below the screen, the field is made to follow a prescribed pattern, and the waves from there cross the slab and meet on the slice. The pattern is worked out from the page: for each frequency, what the faces must do for the field in the middle to add up to the page. Shapes coarser than the wavelength cross the slab as travelling waves; finer detail does not travel at all, but the slab is thin enough that it still reaches the middle before it fades (the near field, which is how microscopes see past the wavelength). The page is held only approximately, with faint rings and coloured fringes where the three frequencies disagree.

When the page changes (it loads, or you scroll and more of it comes into view), the pattern on the faces switches over smoothly and the field carries on from where it is. The change comes in from the faces: fine detail follows them almost at once, while the travelling waves, which carry the colour and the large shapes, overshoot and ring out across the slab. The field moves with the page, so text that has only moved stays as it is; a strip that has just come into view fills in from the faces. The slider in the corner sets the wavelength at 10 Hz, and with it how fast the waves move.
