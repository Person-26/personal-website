How this page is drawn

Every colour here is an electric field. The page is the flat slice z = 0 through an electromagnetic wave in three dimensions, and each pixel's red, green and blue (minus the background) are Ex, Ey and Ez there.

Nothing is drawn directly. The screen is only a window on the slice: past every edge, and above and below the slice, space is open out to the far field. The light is ordinary light: massless, every wave moving at c, and every wave transverse, so Gauss's law holds exactly. The light comes from electric currents. One fills each side margin of the screen, the empty space beside the text, and two current sheets lie parallel to the slice, a little above and below it. The currents are shaped so that they send waves only inward, toward the text, timed so that they all fall into step on the slice four seconds after they start. So nothing appears outside the margins while a page is forming, and at first nothing at all, until the light from the sheets has reached the slice. A wave tilted out of the slice can carry what a flat one cannot: the field along its own direction of travel in the plane, and Ez. So those parts of the colour arrive on steeper paths and only sharpen in close to the moment the page forms.

When you scroll, the field moves with the page, so text already formed slides along unchanged and out of the window. But the waves were held in step where the text used to be. A page that has formed is not stored anywhere: it is light flowing steadily through the slice from the sheets, every pattern carried by waves crossing at its own angle. When the currents switch to the page as it now is, the old light still on its way finishes crossing and then it is gone: broad shapes first, whose waves cross head-on, fine detail last, whose waves come in at grazing angles. (Waves still on their way when a page is replaced keep going: they focus and then spread out of the slice and off the screen.) Meanwhile the new page comes in the same way, and until it focuses you see the waves on their way, crossing the screen and interfering. The simulation runs on a grid coarser than your screen, so once an area visibly matches the page it is forming (judged by comparing the two images, not the field), it slowly fades over to the same page drawn at full resolution, and fades back if new waves come through.
