How this page is drawn

Everything you see here is light, simulated. The screen is a wave field, and the field is a mix of three frequencies: those of blue (460 nm), green (540 nm) and red (620 nm) light, slowed down so that the middle of the visible range would be 10 Hz. Three are enough, as for a laser projector: the eye has only three kinds of colour receptor. Each pixel's colour is what those frequencies look like together: how strongly each one oscillates there, seen through the eye's sensitivity to colour. The white background is all three at once; dark text is where there is no light.

The screen is the middle of a slab of space eight wavelengths thick, filled with that field. Every frequency moves at the same speed, so each has its own wavelength. Nothing on the screen is drawn or driven directly. The only input is a boundary condition on the slab's two faces, above and below the screen: each face sends in a prescribed wave and lets through whatever reaches it. The waves cross the slab and meet in the middle, and where they meet, their interference is the page: a standing pattern that holds still while the light in it oscillates.

The wave each face sends is worked out from the page: for each frequency, which waves must cross the slab to add up to the page in the middle. Only shapes coarser than about half a wavelength can be carried that far, so the page is held only approximately, with faint rings and coloured fringes where the three frequencies disagree. The slab is uniform, so rather than stepping every point of it forward in time, each pattern's crossing is solved exactly, including the ringing front that arrives first.

When the page changes (it loads, or you scroll and more of it comes into view), the faces do not simply switch to the new wave: that change would cross the slab straight on and appear everywhere at once, as a fade. Instead they send the new page out of focus first, focused far beyond the screen, then pull the focus in over about three seconds. Out of focus, a page is its own diffraction pattern: fringes and rings around every edge. And the light is slanted, the left half of the page carried by waves leaning right and the right half by waves leaning left, so out of focus each half sits off to its own side. As the focus arrives the two halves travel in from left and right, their fringes wash over what is already there, and the rings close into the text. The field moves with the page, so text that has only moved stays as it is. The slider in the corner sets the wavelength at 10 Hz: shorter is sharper.
